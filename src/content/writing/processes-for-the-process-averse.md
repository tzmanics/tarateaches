Say the word "process" to a room of engineers and watch the joy leave their bodies. They're not wrong to flinch — most of them have survived process that existed to generate the *feeling* of control: status meetings about status meetings, tickets about tickets, Gantt charts nobody could read and nobody missed.

I gave a whole conference talk about this, because I've spent years introducing process to teams that hated process — and getting thanked for it. The trick isn't selling process harder. It's building process that passes three tests.

## Test 1: It removes a pain someone already feels

Never introduce process to prevent a hypothetical. Introduce it to kill a pain the team already complains about. "We keep getting surprised by template breakage" becomes a scheduled check. "Nobody knows who owns this" becomes an ownership doc. When process is the answer to *their* complaint, adoption is free.

If you can't name the pain a process removes, that process is decoration. Cut it.

## Test 2: It costs less than the problem

Every process has a price paid in attention. A daily 30-minute sync to prevent a monthly 30-minute confusion is a terrible trade, and your team can do that math even when you can't. My rule: prototype the cheapest version first — a checklist before a tool, a doc before a meeting, a meeting before a role.

## Test 3: It survives your vacation

Process that requires its author to enforce it isn't process, it's supervision with paperwork. The version that works is the version the team runs when you're not looking — which means they have to understand *why* it exists, not just *that* it exists. I write the why at the top of every process doc. When the why stops being true, anyone is allowed to propose killing the process. Some of my proudest process work is process I deleted.

## The high-level individualization part

Teams aren't interchangeable, and neither are the humans on them. The same workflow lands differently on a team of five seniors versus a team absorbing three new grads. Process should flex around the people — cadence, ceremony, documentation depth — while the *why* stays fixed. That's the balance: individualize the mechanics, standardize the reasons.

Momentum is precious. Good process is how you protect it — not the tax you charge against it.
