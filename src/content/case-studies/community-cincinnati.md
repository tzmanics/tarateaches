## The situation

Cincinnati had developers who wanted to learn and developers who wanted to teach, and almost no structure connecting them — especially for women and career-changers who'd been burned by rooms where the first question is a test.

So I built the rooms.

## What I actually did

Launched the Cincinnati chapter of Women Who Code and the Cincinnati branch of NodeSchool, and ran them the way I think all education should run:

- Recurring and reliable — a meetup that happens every month beats a conference that happens once.
- Mentor-dense — enough helpers in the room that getting stuck lasted minutes, not the whole night.
- Zero hazing — no trick questions, no "you don't know that?", no gatekeeping dressed up as standards.

## What it took

The unglamorous parts of community building: venues, sponsors, pizza logistics, no-show speakers, and showing up on months when showing up was hard. And the discipline to make myself less necessary — recruiting co-organizers, documenting how everything ran, and handing over the keys on purpose.

## What came out of it

Chapters that kept running after I stepped back — which is the only community-building metric I fully trust. People who walked in as beginners and came back as mentors, then organizers. And a conviction I've carried into every education role since: if your program collapses without its founder, you didn't build a program, you built a personality.
