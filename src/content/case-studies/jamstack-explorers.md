## The situation

Netlify wanted a learning platform: free, video-first courses ("missions") teaching modern web development, taught by a roster of creators from across the community — not one in-house instructor. Multiple creators meant multiple recording setups, teaching styles, opinions about what "done" means, and calendars that never lined up.

My job as tech lead: ship the platform, and make a dozen independent creators feel like one coherent curriculum.

## What I actually did

The engineering was the easy half — mission structure, progress tracking, and a certification path with completion criteria that meant something. The real work was the invisible tooling and process around the creators:

- A repeatable mission template so every course had the same shape: learning objectives up front, hands-on checkpoints, a deployable artifact at the end.
- Creator onboarding that treated educators like collaborators, not vendors — clear specs, honest feedback loops, and deadlines with padding built in because life happens.
- Review passes that protected the learner: if a mission drifted from its stated objectives, we caught it before a learner paid for that drift with their time.

## What it took

Coordinating engineers, educators, designers, and marketing toward one launch date — and then the less glamorous part: keeping creators shipping *after* launch, when the excitement was gone and everyone had day jobs again. That's where process earned its keep: lightweight enough that nobody dreaded it, visible enough that nothing silently stalled.

## What came out of it

A certification learners actually finished, a mission format other teams borrowed, and a working model for multi-creator education: one spine, many voices. It's the project that convinced me curriculum architecture is a leadership problem wearing an engineering costume.
